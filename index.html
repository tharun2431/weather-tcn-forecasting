How this forecast is made

A recurrent neural network (LSTM) runs entirely in your browser via ONNX Runtime — no server, and it works offline once loaded. It reads the last 168 hours of conditions (14 derived variables including potential temperature, vapour pressure and air density) and predicts hour by hour.

The shaded band is a conformal prediction interval calibrated per forecast hour, so roughly 90 out of 100 actual temperatures should fall inside it. Widths come from the dissertation's calibration: ±1.26 °C at 1 hour, widening to ±4.81 °C at 24 hours.

The in-browser model was trained on a single city (Jena) and is applied to all five here — a practical test of the cross-city generalisation question this project studies.

Same day, five different shapes

Each panel is the coming 24 hours on its own temperature scale, so you're comparing the shape of the day rather than who is warmest. Peaks land at different hours, and the daily swing is far larger in some cities than others.

A pooled model has to reproduce all five of these patterns with one set of weights. That it manages to — matching five specialised models — is the central result here.

Climate range

Why these five

Daily temperatures in Jena, London, New York and Tokyo move together (correlation +0.81 to +0.90). Sydney runs opposite — about −0.8 against all of them — because its seasons are reversed.

That contrast is the point: it makes "does pooling cities help?" a question worth asking rather than a foregone conclusion.

One pooled model trained on all five cities matches five separate per-city models almost exactly. Pooling doesn't cost accuracy — and leaves one model to maintain instead of five.
